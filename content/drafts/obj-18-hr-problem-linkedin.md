"Wellness is HR's problem."

I hear a version of this from operations and finance leaders. It's honest, and it's exactly why so many programs stall.

Here's where the cost actually lands.

When a burned-out engineer resigns, HR runs the search. But the delay to the project, the overtime the rest of the team absorbs, and the six months before the replacement is fully productive all land on the line manager's budget and the operations plan.

Replacing one employee costs 6–9 months of their salary. (SHRM) HR doesn't pay most of that. The business does.

Same with sick days. Burned-out employees are 63% more likely to take one. (Gallup) HR records the absence. The team covers the work.

So when wellness sits only in HR's budget, it's being funded by the department that sees the least of the cost and evaluated by people who never see the savings.

The fix isn't a bigger HR budget. It's a different conversation: put the turnover and absence cost next to the line managers who carry it, and let them see the participation numbers for their own teams.

When an ops leader can see that half their team hasn't engaged with anything in a month, it stops being HR's problem.

Who in your company actually pays when someone burns out?
