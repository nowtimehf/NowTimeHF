$300 billion. That's the annual bill U.S. employers pay for workplace stress, according to the American Institute of Stress.

It's a number so large it stops meaning anything. So let's shrink it.

Spread across the U.S. workforce, $300B works out to roughly $1,800 per employee, per year.

Run a 150-person company? That's about $270,000 walking out the door annually — in absenteeism, lost productivity, and turnover you are already paying for.

You won't find it as a line item. It's buried:

▸ Burned-out employees are 63% more likely to take a sick day.
▸ They are 23% more likely to end up in the emergency room.
▸ Both land on your insurance renewal. Neither shows up labeled "burnout."

Here's what makes this a current-quarter problem instead of a someday problem: you are probably already spending on wellness. The industry average program sees 6–8% employee participation. You are funding access that 92% of your people never touch, then absorbing the full cost of the stress it was supposed to reduce.

That is not an employee motivation problem. That is a program design problem.

And behind every one of those percentages is somebody on your team running on empty. That is the real reason to fix this. The P&L case just makes it urgent.
