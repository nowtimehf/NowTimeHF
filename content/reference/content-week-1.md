## Mon — LinkedIn — Cost of Inaction

76% of employees experience burnout at work at least sometimes. 28% feel it very often or always. (Gallup)

Here's the part HR budgets miss:

Replacing just one of them costs 6–9 months of their salary. (SHRM)

For a 100-person company with average salaries of $70k and typical turnover, that's not a wellness problem.

That's a six-figure line item hiding in plain sight.

Most companies respond with a gym stipend nobody uses and a meditation app nobody opens.

Then they wonder why the number doesn't move.

Burnout isn't fixed by perks. It's fixed by systems — daily structure, real accountability, and support employees actually engage with.

Question for the HR leaders here: do you know your wellness benefit's actual utilization rate?

If the answer is "not really" — that's the first number worth finding.

## Tue — LinkedIn — Proof & Practice

3 years ago a client told me he hadn't slept past 5am in a decade.

Not because of discipline. Because of stress.

He was a director at a mid-size company. High performer. The guy leadership never worried about.

We didn't start with workouts. We started with 3 things:

→ A hard stop to his workday
→ 20 minutes of movement before email
→ One weekly check-in where someone actually asked how he was doing

90 days later he was sleeping 7 hours, down 14 lbs, and — his words — "actually present" in meetings.

Here's what his company never saw: they were 6 weeks away from losing him.

Your highest performers don't complain. They leave.

The companies that keep them are the ones paying attention before the resignation letter, not after.

## Wed — LinkedIn — Contrarian / Cost of Inaction

Your wellness program isn't failing because employees are lazy.

It's failing because it was designed for a brochure, not a human.

Free gym membership. Meditation app. Fruit basket on Tuesdays.

Looks great in the benefits deck. Changes almost nothing.

Because here's the truth from 15 years on the gym floor: people don't fail from lack of access.

They fail from lack of structure and accountability.

Nobody skips the gym because the door was locked. They skip it because no one notices when they do.

Real numbers back this up: only 42% of burned-out employees ever tell their manager. And of those who do, 42% say nothing changes. (Mind Share Partners)

Access isn't the product. Follow-through is.

If your wellness spend isn't building daily structure and human accountability, you're not buying wellness.

You're buying a brochure.

## Thu — Facebook — Practical Tip

Try this before your next stressful meeting:

4 seconds in through your nose. Hold for 4. 4 seconds out. Repeat 4 times.

That's it. Box breathing — the same tool we used in the Marines to stay sharp under pressure.

60 seconds. No app. No equipment. Works in a parking lot, a hallway, or on mute in a Zoom call.

Drop a ✅ below after you try it today — I want to hear where you used it.

## Thu — LinkedIn — Practical Framework

The 3-question audit I run before any company works with us:

1. What's your wellness benefit's actual utilization rate? (Not enrollment. Usage. Most leaders have never seen this number.)
2. When did you last ask employees what support they'd actually use? (A survey from 2023 doesn't count.)
3. Who follows up when someone disengages? (If the answer is "no one," that's your program's real design flaw.)

Companies that can answer all 3 rarely need me.

Companies that can't are usually spending real money on a program that's quietly doing nothing — while turnover does the damage. And turnover isn't cheap: 6–9 months of salary per departure. (SHRM)

Run the audit on your own program this week. The answers tell you everything.
